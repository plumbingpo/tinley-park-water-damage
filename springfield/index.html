What to Do Before Our Springfield Crew Arrives

People mean well in the first hour after a leak, and some of what they do makes things worse. These are the mistakes our crews see most often in Springfield, and what to do instead.

Do not use a household vacuum on water. It is an electric shock risk and it will ruin the machine; use towels and a mop. Do not turn on ceiling fans or lights under a wet ceiling, and stay out of rooms where the ceiling is sagging. Do not lift tacked-down carpet yourself, since it can shrink or tear and may be saveable if it is handled properly. Avoid cranking up the heating, as warmth plus moisture encourages mold.

The helpful things are simple: close the valve, prop open cupboards and wardrobes, and stand wet cushions and textiles up to drain. Photograph each room before you shift a single item. Keep kids and animals out until it has been checked. And do make the call early, so Tinley Park Restoration can have a crew on the way to Springfield while you do the rest.

What Usually Goes Wrong in Springfield Homes

In Springfield, the same handful of problems accounts for nearly all the water damage we see. If you can spot these early, you have a good chance of stopping the damage before it spreads.

Pipes: When water freezes inside a line it can crack the metal or plastic, then pour out as soon as things warm up. Depending on the season, the plumbing most at risk runs through outside walls, attics and garages without heat. Corroded fittings and worn supply lines can also let go without any warning in older homes. Shut off the main valve right away, because an open line can soak several rooms quickly.

Appliances: Washing machines, dishwashers and refrigerator ice makers all depend on small hoses that wear out. A water heater near the end of its life can leak from the tank bottom or fail all at once. These failures do the most harm when the appliance is on an upper floor or above finished living space.

Overflows: A clogged toilet or a sink left running can put water on the floor in minutes. The water often runs under baseboards and through the ceiling of the room below. Water that backed up out of a drain or toilet bowl is not clean, so keep kids and pets out of it.

Roof: Missing shingles, cracked flashing and clogged gutters let rain into the attic. By the time a brown ring appears overhead, the attic insulation has often been wet for a while. We dry the framing, insulation and drywall, and a roofer needs to fix the opening so it does not return.

Basement: Once the ground outside is soaked, water finds its way into a basement through wall cracks and the seam where floor meets wall. If the sump pump sticks or the electricity goes out mid-storm, the pit fills and spills across the slab. Carpet, wall studs and drywall wick moisture upward, which is why finished lower levels take the worst of it.

Mold: Mold can start growing within 24 to 48 hours on materials that stay wet. Many mold jobs we handle in Springfield began with an old leak that was wiped up but never dried inside the wall. Have it looked at if you notice a stale odor, bubbling paint or dark specks around an earlier leak.

Drying Out a Springfield Property: What to Expect

Touch is a poor test for whether a wall or floor has dried. The face of drywall often seems normal when the cavity side is damp, and carpet fibers dry long before the padding underneath. For that reason we rely on meters, not on feel. In Springfield we use pin meters that measure moisture inside wood and drywall, non-invasive meters that scan behind surfaces, and thermal cameras that show cool, damp patches.

Different materials let go of water at different speeds. Carpet and drywall respond quickly, while framing lumber, subfloor, hardwood and concrete hold on much longer. The layout of dehumidifiers and air movers follows those rates and is changed as each day's numbers come in. Most jobs finish in 3 to 5 days, and if yours will take longer you will hear it from us at the start.

The expensive error is pulling equipment too soon. Damp materials sealed behind new finishes may mold, buckle or delaminate months afterward. Every Springfield job is therefore dried to a recorded target, and the log stays on file.
